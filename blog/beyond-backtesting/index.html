How to Validate AI Agents Beyond Backtesting

A backtest is a take-home exam. It tells you the agent learned something. It does not tell you the agent can apply the knowledge in real-time.

By Roostoo Team · 12 Nov 2026

You’ve probably seen it: someone shares a screenshot of a trading bot’s backtest — a magnificently smooth line going up and to the right. Then they turn it on with real money, and the line goes south.

Quantopian once studied 888 trading algorithms from its users and found that backtest performance explained less than 2.5% of live results. Worse, the more a developer backtested, the bigger the gap became.

A backtest is like a take-home exam. It tells you the agent learned something. It does not tell you the agent can apply the knowledge well in real-time.

Let’s dive into two ways a backtest can mislead.

Problem 1: Curve-fitting (or overfitting)

The backtest assumes historical patterns will repeat. Live markets show that most of those patterns were noise.

Researchers Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado, and Zhu showed that high simulated performance is easy to achieve after testing only a modest number of configurations. Overfit strategies can carry negative expected returns out of sample — and the more configurations tested, the worse the out-of-sample results tend to be.

Problem 2: Peeking at the answers

Sometimes backtesting lets the agent see the future by accident — like a feature that uses a bar’s closing price before the bar has actually closed. The agent looks perfectly timed in the backtest. In real life, that information doesn’t exist yet.

So, how do we do better?

The answer is a trading simulator with live prices and forward-testing on data unknown in advance. An agent can’t overfit to or cheat on data that didn’t exist when it was built.

That’s why we built Roostoo’s live paper trading competitions. We backtest agents to filter out obvious failures, then users deploy them into live competitions where they trade on prices nobody has seen yet.

We judge live agents on three things:

  1. Robustness — whether weaker runs still do okay
  2. Capital protection — how shallow the worst drawdown is
  3. Profit probability — whether profit is consistent or concentrated in a few large moves

The agent trust ladder

  • A great backtest — low trust
  • One great live competition — low trust
  • Ten or more competitions across different markets — medium trust
  • Consistent wins with shallow drawdowns — high trust

Real Mode: capital

Even then, paper isn’t real. Liquidity, slippage, funding, and latency all differ on a real venue.

So the next step is trading with Real Mode. You start with a small ticket of real capital and set the guards: ticket limit, stop-loss, take-profit, and max leverage. You keep custody of your wallet keys.

Agents lose competitions. Real trading can lose real money. The guardrails are limits you choose to mitigate your risk.
